Resume

I’m Yoon. I lead UX research and strategy at pxd in Seoul, and the team of researchers and strategists that does it.

Much of my work begins while the problem is still unclear. The evidence does not match the brief, stakeholders want different things, or we’re designing something people haven’t experienced before. Working that out means moving between the four in the navigation: people, business, system, technology.

I’m based in Vancouver and work remotely with my team in Seoul. The list of things I want to find out hasn’t got any shorter, which is most of why I keep doing this.

People

Understanding what I have not lived

I have never run a semiconductor line or operated an MRI scanner. When I have to understand a situation I have never been in, it starts with meeting people I do not know. Sitting with them, watching the work, asking what it is actually like. So the first question is not what to ask, but who can answer.

01
Learn from current users

Meet people who already use the product, or something close to it. Watch them as well as ask. What they say is real evidence, and it is rarely all of it. The need worth building for usually sits behind the wording, in a pause, a face, the thing nobody bothers to mention.

02
Make the users

Sometimes the people a product is aimed at are not interested, or turn it down outright. That is a finding, not a conclusion. Give them the product long enough to live with it. The real problems and the real openings show up in what they do.

03
Read the outliers

A product aimed years out has no users yet. So I go to the edge cases in a nearby industry: someone unreasonably careful about the security of their money, someone with strong opinions about the cup holders in their car.

Business

Evidence is not a decision

Research can change how a team sees a problem. Deciding what to do about it is a different job. I turn findings into priorities, product direction, and trade-offs people have actually agreed to. The hard part is when the best thing for the user costs the business.

01
Co-create the opportunities

I develop opportunities with the people who would have to build and deliver them. Bringing them in early means the constraints show up while the ideas are still cheap to change. Weak options die in the room, not in a review three months later.

02
Map the exchange

I map what each party contributes and what it receives: data, revenue, physical assets, access to customers. That shows what an idea depends on. Some ideas look strong until the map puts the company in a role it cannot fill.

03
Agree the basis for choosing

The criteria depend on the decision. They might be customer need, business fit and feasibility, or something else. I get the team to agree on them before anyone scores anything. Otherwise product, engineering and strategy each argue from the criterion they already favour.

System

Structures other teams can keep using

The research gets done. The results get written up. Then, months later, someone asks: where was that thing? What did it say? I have watched my own work go that way. So I build what we learn the way a design system is built: parts that other teams can take, in a shape that holds up after I leave the project.

01
Break it into atoms

A study produces one deliverable and a lot of small facts. The facts are often what another team needs. So I break the subject into parts: who is using the product, what situation they are using it in, what they are trying to get done, what the technology could offer, and so on. Say the study was about a family of four in a large SUV. The team working on a small sedan can still use parts of it.

02
Make each atom findable

What a team looks for is not only the result of a study. It is the problem that made someone ask in the first place, the material the study was built from, and what changed after it. Each of those is an atom too. They have to sit inside the way the team already works, not in a folder someone has to remember.

Technology

Make new technology useful at work

My work puts me in front of new technology before most people see it. Right now that is almost always AI. The usual job is to make it easy to use, and that matters. Whether people keep using it comes down to something else: whether it reaches the work they already have.

01
Make it easy to understand

The ordinary job, and it still has to be done. People have to be able to tell what the thing does, what it is doing right now, and what to do when it gets something wrong. With AI that last one matters more than it used to.

02
Then connect it to the work

Easy gets someone through the first use. What brings them back is the thing reaching into what they already do: the documents they work from, the systems they work in, the way their organisation expects a piece of work to be done. That is usually where the gap sits.
